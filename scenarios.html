When this happens, try this.

A small set of scenarios you'll actually walk into, with the specific move worth trying. Each one points back to the module step where the move is built out, so you can read the deeper version when you have time.

If your teen blows up at you over something small
Try acknowledging the feeling before anything else.

The move is not to solve the small thing. It is to name the size of the reaction. It sounds like you've had a really hard day beats This is not worth being upset about almost every time. Faber's four-step acknowledgment is the script; Damour's framing of intensity as information is the reason it works.

Module II · Listening Before Talking
If your son has gone quiet and stays quiet
Try talking sideways instead of head-on.

Natterson's most reliable move is to put the conversation in motion. Drive somewhere together. Walk the dog together. Do dishes side by side. No eye contact, no fixed agenda. The words sneak in when nothing else is happening. Boys especially seem to find the angle of approach more important than the words you choose.

Module III · Silence, Shutdown, and Boys
If you're about to ground them for the month
Pause. Reach for an alternative first.

Punishment rarely produces the change you actually want. Faber and Mazlish offer five alternatives that do: state your feelings, state your expectations, show how to make amends, offer a choice, take action. Damour reframes it the same way in audio form. The pause is the move. The alternative comes after the pause.

Module V · Conflict, Limits, and Repair
If your kid seems to care more about their friends than about you
Don't compete. Be the relationship that doesn't change.

This shift is developmental, not personal. Neufeld and Maté make the case (a polemical one, worth pushing back on) that the gap between peer-orientation and adult attachment has widened in the last generation. Whether you fully buy the thesis or not, the practical move is the same: keep showing up in small, regular ways. A meal. A walk. Ten minutes at bedtime. The relationship grows in those.

Module IV · Friendship and Belonging
If you blew it during a fight with them
Go back. The repair is the move that builds the relationship.

Siegel calls this returning home. Name what happened, take responsibility for the part that was yours, and ask what would help. No but. No relitigating the original conflict. The rupture isn't the problem; the absence of repair is. Most parents can manage the repair sentence. Most parents never quite get around to it.

Module V · Conflict, Limits, and Repair
If you can't tell whether they're struggling or in real trouble
Use Damour's three-question diagnostic.

Ask yourself: is the level of distress proportionate to what's happened? Does it pass on its own? Is the kid still functioning in the rest of life? If yes to all three, you're likely looking at ordinary distress, and your job is steady scaffolding. If no to any of them, it's time to bring in a professional. The test is a lot more useful than waiting for the situation to declare itself.

When a kid is monosyllabic at their desk
Don't demand speech. Make small invitations they can refuse.

A passing comment with no follow-up does more work than a check-in conversation. Glad you're here today. Then move on. Damour's chapter on regaining emotional control distinguishes healthy quiet (a kid regulating) from harmful quiet (a kid sealing off). Most classroom silence is the first kind. The work is to keep the door cracked, not to pry it open.

When a kid is seeking attention disruptively
Name the feeling once, quietly. Then move on.

The disruption is usually about belonging, not authority. A brief acknowledgment defuses faster than escalation. I can see you're frustrated. Let's talk after class. The move borrows Faber's first acknowledgment step and Fagell's instinct to translate raw behavior into something nameable. After class, the conversation might be a real one.

When a student keeps not turning in homework
Run Faber's five-step problem-solving instead of escalating consequences.

Bring it up calmly. Listen to their side first, before anything else. Share your view. Brainstorm together. Write down what you both agreed to. The five steps reliably outperform punitive consequences because the kid leaves with a plan they helped build, not a punishment they're stewing about. The first time you run it cold, it feels awkward. The second time it doesn't.

Module V · Conflict, Limits, and Repair
When a kid melts down in class
Drop the lesson for thirty seconds. Acknowledge before redirecting.

The first words out of your mouth are not calm down and not everyone, eyes back to the board. They are something close to I can see this is really hard right now. Take a minute. The thirty seconds you spend doing this saves the next ten you'd otherwise spend pulling the room back together. Damour's framing of distress as information is what makes the pause feel sane.

When a student stops trying
Ask what's underneath. Not why they aren't applying themselves.

Disengagement is almost never about the work. It's a friendship rupture, a family situation, an undiagnosed learning difficulty, or one specific class period that's gone bad. Fagell's chapter on intervening when school is a struggle gives you a working set of what to say moves. Try the curious-not-frustrated version: I've noticed you've been quiet in here lately. Is there something I should know?

When two friends are fighting and one is in your classroom
Be curious. Don't arbitrate.

You don't have to resolve the rift. You don't have power over the other half of it anyway. Help the kid in front of you name what's happening, decide what they want to do next, and rehearse the actual sentence they could use. Fagell's Chapter 6 is built around this exact move. The kid usually doesn't need a solution from you; they need help locating their own.
