Module V · Friction

Conflict, Limits, and Repair

How to follow through without making the situation worse

Essential Question

How do I hold a line without breaking the relationship?

Reading ~80 min Listening ~95 min Check tasks ~25 min Total ~3.25 hours

Module II taught what to say in the first thirty seconds of a hard conversation. Module III taught what to do when nothing you say is landing. This module is for the third situation, the one neither of the others handles: a real rule has been broken, a real limit needs to hold, and you have to do something. The instinct most adults reach for is some form of punishment, because that's what their own parents and teachers did. The result is usually some combination of three things. The teen complies temporarily but learns nothing about the underlying behavior. The relationship absorbs a small cut that doesn't fully heal. Or both.

The point of this module isn't to make you a softer disciplinarian. It is to give you specific moves that hold the line and protect the relationship at the same time. Those are not at odds, and the work of this module is the work of believing that.

The Thesis of the Module

Punishment tells the kid you're displeased. Accountability tells the kid what to do differently. Most adults try the first hoping it produces the second. It rarely does. The hard, useful work happens between the broken rule and the consequence, in the move most adults skip.

Each step below follows the Chunk · Chew · Check format. Don't skip the Chew or the Check. They're where the philosophy turns into a sentence you can say in real time.

1
Step One · Listen First

Joanna Faber and Julie King, in conversation ~50 min

Joanna Faber & Julie King · Dr. Robyn Silverman’s How to Talk to Kids About Anything podcast · "How to Talk When Kids Won't Listen" · listen at drrobynsilverman.com

Joanna Faber is Adele Faber's daughter, and she has carried her mother's work forward for the last two decades with co-author Julie King. This episode is the cleanest contemporary statement of the alternatives-to-punishment approach you'll find in audio. Silverman is the right interviewer for it, because she keeps pulling Faber and King back to the specific moment a parent or teacher is in when the punishment instinct shows up.

Chunk

Listen to the full episode (about fifty minutes). It's a casual three-way conversation, so commute pace is fine.

Chew

Mark the moments where Faber and King distinguish between punishment (designed to make the kid feel bad about themselves) and the alternative moves Faber Senior laid out fifty years ago. Notice especially how they handle the moment where the parent's instinct is to escalate. The escalation instinct is the thing this whole module is asking you to interrupt.

Check

Write a single sentence you could say in the heat of the moment instead of "go to your room" or "I'm taking your phone." Make it under ten words. Make it useful. Put it on a sticky note where you'll see it.

2
Step Two · Read · the core text

Five alternatives and five steps ~45 min

Adele Faber & Elaine Mazlish · How to Talk So Teens Will Listen · Chapter 3 ("To Punish or Not to Punish") and Chapter 4 ("Working It Out Together"). Both chapters are short and the cartoon panels do a lot of the work; budget less time than the page count suggests.

Faber and Mazlish do the hardest work of the book in these two chapters. Chapter 3 names five concrete alternatives to punishment, with the wrong-way and right-way cartoon panels that are their signature. Chapter 4 walks through a five-step problem-solving conversation in which the teen is a partner rather than a defendant. Read them in order. Chapter 3 will feel like a list of small moves; Chapter 4 will show you how they fit together.

You can hold a line and hold the relationship. They are not opposites. Most of the time, they are the same move.
Chunk

Read Chapter 3 (five alternatives to punishment) and Chapter 4 (the five-step problem-solving move). The cartoon panels are doing more work than they look like they are; read them slowly.

Chew

Mark the five alternatives in Chapter 3. Mark the five steps in Chapter 4. Notice how much overlap there is between them. Most of the work, in both chapters, is acknowledging the feeling underneath the broken rule and inviting the teen into the search for what to do differently. Punishment forecloses both of those moves. The alternatives keep them open.

Check

Pick a real recurring conflict in your classroom or your kitchen. Write out, on paper, how you'd run Faber's five-step problem-solving move on it. Even if you never run it in real life, rehearsing once is the only way to have any chance of running it cold when the real moment comes. The first try is always uneven. That's not a sign you should give up on the move; it's a sign you were honest with yourself about what changing this part of your practice actually takes.

3
Step Three · Read · the complement

Repair is the move that builds the relationship ~35 min

Daniel Siegel · Brainstorm: The Power and Purpose of the Teenage Brain · Part IV ("Staying Present Through Changes and Challenges"), especially the section "Returning Home: Reflection, Realignment, and Repairing Ruptures."

Faber gives you the moves to avoid the rupture in the first place. Siegel takes the moment that comes next: the rupture that happened anyway. His argument here is that repair is the part that builds the relationship, not the part that fixes it. Ruptures are unavoidable in any close relationship with an adolescent. The question is whether the adult goes back.

Chunk

Read Part IV. The introductory pages on presence are worth a careful read. Skim the broader chapters on identity, sexuality, and leaving home unless one of them speaks to a current question. Concentrate on the material on returning home and repairing ruptures.

Chew

Mark Siegel's specific guidance on what to do when YOU are the one who blew it. Notice how often his advice involves the adult naming what happened, taking responsibility for the part that was theirs, and asking what would help. None of those moves involve saying "but" or relitigating the original conflict. That's not an accident; it's the architecture of repair.

Check

Think of a recent rupture with a student or one of your own kids. Write three sentences. First: what happened. Second: what you did, or didn't do, at the time. Third: the repair sentence you could go to them with this week. Then go say the third one. Don't rehearse it past one or two passes. The repair doesn't have to be polished; it has to be honest.

4
Step Four · Optional · the deeper cut

Damour on consequence versus punishment ~45 min

Lisa Damour & Reena Ninan · Ask Lisa: The Psychology of Raising Tweens & Teens · Episode 170, "How Should I Deal with My Angry, Disrespectful Son?" · listen at drlisadamour.com

Damour's specific argument in this episode is that traditional punishment with teens is less effective than asking them to repair what they damaged. The episode is built around a parent's question, but the framework applies just as cleanly to a classroom moment. About forty-five minutes.

Chunk

Listen to the full episode.

Chew

Notice Damour's careful separation of consequences (the logical result of a behavior, which the teen has to address) from punishment (suffering imposed because the behavior was bad). The first teaches. The second rarely does. Listen for the specific sentence Damour suggests parents say: something close to "your consequence is, you're going to have to repair it."

Check

Write your own one-paragraph discipline philosophy, in your own voice, that you could hand to a co-teacher or a co-parent. Keep it under a hundred words. The constraint matters; a long discipline philosophy is the kind of thing nobody reads, including the version of you that needs it most.

Module Check · The Big Question

The next time you feel the urge to punish

Pause. Ask yourself: am I trying to teach this kid something specific, or am I trying to make them feel bad for what they did? If it's the first, what is the specific thing? If it's the second, that's punishment, and Faber and Damour and Siegel all agree that it rarely produces the change you want. The pause itself is the move this module is asking for.

The hardest part of this module is letting yourself trust that holding a line and holding the relationship aren't opposites. The instinct to punish is, in most adults, more about adult feelings (frustration, hurt pride, fear of looking soft) than about the kid's behavior. The moves in this module take those adult feelings seriously and ask you to handle them somewhere other than on the kid. That is harder than it sounds. It is also the practice this work asks for.

Module VI takes the last step. Every module before this one has been the adult literature on teens. The last module flips that and listens to the only people who actually know what it's like to be in your classroom.
