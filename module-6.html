Module VI · Practice

What Students Actually Want from Us

The most useful PD is a kid telling you what made the difference

Essential Question

What do kids actually want from the adults in their lives?

Reading ~80 min Listening ~60 min Check tasks ~25 min Total ~2.75 hours

This is the last module, and it's the smallest. Not because the topic is unimportant. Because the work of this module is mostly listening rather than reading.

Every module before this one has drawn from the adult literature on teenagers: what the neuroscientists say, what the clinicians say, what the veteran practitioners say. The single book in this module is different. It hands the microphone back to the only people who actually know what it's like to be in your classroom on a Tuesday morning, sitting in the third row, watching the clock. The middle schoolers themselves.

This is the module to take slowly. Not because of the page count, but because of how slowly the material lands. A teacher reading their twentieth Damour chapter can absorb it on a commute. A teacher reading three pages of fourteen-year-olds talking about teachers needs to put the book down at least once a page.

The Thesis of the Module

The most useful PD you'll ever have isn't a workshop. It's a kid telling you what made the difference. This module takes that seriously by handing the microphone to actual middle schoolers and asking the adults to be quiet for a while.

Each step below follows the Chunk · Chew · Check format. Don't skip the Chew or the Check. They're where listening turns into something you can carry into the next hard moment.

1
Step One · Listen First

Phyllis Fagell on the middle school years ~60 min

Phyllis Fagell · Psychologists Off the Clock podcast · Episode 272, "Middle School Matters with Phyllis Fagell" · listen at offtheclockpsych.com

Fagell has worked as a middle school counselor for a long time and has talked to thousands of kids who would never sit in front of a microphone. Her translation of student voice into specific teacher moves is the bridge this whole module rests on. Start here.

Chunk

Listen to the full episode (about an hour). Fagell is a careful, specific speaker; the time goes faster than you'd expect.

Chew

Listen for the moments where Fagell translates something she has heard from a kid into a specific teacher move. That translation, from student voice to adult action, is the model for the rest of the module. Notice that her translations are almost never sweeping. They're small and concrete. A sentence. A pause. A change in seating. The smallness is the point.

Check

Write down three things Fagell says kids need from adults that you don't currently do, or that you do unevenly. This isn't a guilt list. It's a noticing list. Pick one of the three to try in the next week.

2
Step Two · Read · the core text

Middle schoolers, in their own words ~55 min

Kathleen Cushman & Laura Rogers · Fires in the Middle School Bathroom: Advice for Teachers from Middle Schoolers · Chapter 2 ("A Teacher on Our Side") and Chapter 4 ("Helping Us Grow into Confident Learners"). The book is built almost entirely out of direct student quotes; the pages move quickly.

Cushman and Rogers interviewed forty middle schoolers from around the country and built a book out of what they said. Chapter 2 is about what kids want from their teachers as people. Chapter 4 is about what kids need from their teachers in the moments academic work gets hard. Together they're as close to a focus group on your own classroom as you'll get without running one.

Listen with the part of yourself that doesn't have an answer ready. That part is the part of teaching that gets the lightest use, and the heaviest workout, all at once.
Chunk

Read Chapters 2 and 4. The text is built around direct quotations from middle schoolers; let those carry the chapter rather than the connective prose between them.

Chew

Read with a pen. Underline every line that sounds like a student you actually have in class. Notice where the same theme comes up from kids with very different backgrounds. The patterns matter. The lone outlier voices matter too. Resist the urge to mentally rebut what you read. The work of this chapter is to take the quotes as data, not as opinions.

Check

Pick the three student quotes that hit you hardest. Copy them onto an index card by hand. Tape the card somewhere you'll see it on the way into your classroom for a week. By Friday, decide if you want to keep it up another week or if it's done its job.

3
Step Three · Read · the complement

The bridge between hearing it and doing something ~25 min

Phyllis Fagell · Middle School Matters: The 10 Key Skills Kids Need to Thrive · Chapter 13 ("Intervening When School Is a Struggle"). The chapter's key skills include negotiating conflict, building homework systems, and self-advocacy.

Where Cushman and Rogers give you the raw student voice, Fagell translates it into adult moves. The pairing matters. Student voice without a translation is just an interesting read; the translation without the student voice is one more piece of professional advice that doesn't quite stick to a real Tuesday.

Chunk

Read the chapter in full. It's short and concrete.

Chew

This chapter is the bridge between the student quotes in Step 2 and a teacher move you can run in real time. Mark every "what to say" suggestion Fagell makes. There are more of them in this chapter than there are in most of the books in this library combined; she writes for the practitioner who needs a script tomorrow morning, not a philosophy by the end of the school year.

Check

Find one of the Cushman student quotes you underlined in Step 2 that named a specific need. Find the closest Fagell move to that need. Write the actual sentence you'd say in response, the next time you saw that need in a kid in your room. Make it short. Make it usable.

4
Step Four · Optional · the deeper cut

The archive that picks up where the book left off ~30 min

What Kids Can Do (Cushman’s nonprofit) · "Voices from the Middle Grades" · browse at whatkidscando.org

What Kids Can Do is Kathleen Cushman's nonprofit and it has spent twenty years collecting the kind of student voice the book in Step 2 is built from. The "Voices from the Middle Grades" collection is the natural extension of that book, free and web-readable. Don't try to read everything. Browse like you would a museum collection.

Chunk

Spend half an hour browsing the archive. Find one student voice you want to sit with. You'll know it when you read it.

Chew

Notice what's missing from this kind of student voice that adults usually fill in. There's no jargon. No diagnosis. Just description. Mark the rhetorical choices the kids make. What they leave out is often as instructive as what they say.

Check

Write a two-hundred-word piece titled "What I think my students would say about my classroom," in the voice of one of the kids in the archive. Don't show it to anyone. Just write it. The exercise is the point.

Write a two-hundred-word piece titled "What I think my kid would say about how I show up at home," in the voice of one of the kids in the archive. Don't show it to anyone. Just write it. The exercise is the point.

Module Check · The Big Question

Ask one of them

Pick one student in your life right now. Sometime in the next two weeks, ask them, in a quiet moment, what they wish adults knew about being their age. Not as an interview. Not as a lesson. Just as a question while you're driving or doing the dishes or walking back from somewhere. Listen without responding for at least thirty seconds when they answer. Then thank them, and don't say anything else for a while.

This reader started with the question of why teen behavior makes sense, and it ends with the question of what teens themselves want from the adults in their lives. Those two questions turn out to be the same question, asked from opposite sides.

The work in this reader doesn't end with the last page. It starts there. The next time you walk into a classroom or sit down at the dinner table with a teen, you'll have a few extra moves. Use them slowly. Notice which ones land. Throw out the ones that don't. Most of all, keep asking the question this last module asks: what do they actually want from us, today, in this room, on this Tuesday?
