Module II · Communication Base

Listening Before Talking

What to say in the first thirty seconds, when the conversation hasn't actually started yet

Essential Question

What do I say in the first thirty seconds of a hard conversation?

Reading ~70 min Listening ~30 min Check tasks ~25 min Total ~2 hours

The most common version of this question is the wrong question. Parents and teachers ask, "what do I say?" The honest answer is that the first thirty seconds are not for saying anything that solves the problem. They are for proving you heard the feeling underneath the problem. If that move lands, almost everything you try next gets easier. If it doesn't land, nothing you try next will work.

This module is light on theory and heavy on scripts. You should finish it with actual sentences in your pocket. Not a posture, not a philosophy. Sentences.

The Thesis of the Module

The first thirty seconds of a hard conversation are not for fixing anything. They are for showing the kid that you heard the feeling. Most adult moves fail because they skip that step and jump straight to advice, denial, or a lecture. Acknowledgment is not weakness. It is the only move that opens the door at all.

Each step below follows the Chunk · Chew · Check format. Don't skip the Chew or the Check. They're where the listening turns into something you can actually use on Monday.

1
Step One · Listen First

Adele Faber on acknowledging feelings ~30 min

Adele Faber · The Psych Files podcast · Episode 135, "Adele Faber Interview on Parenting, Part 1" with host Michael Britt · audio and notes at thepsychfiles.com

Start with Faber's voice rather than her book. The interview is rooted in her earlier general work, but the four moves she walks Britt through are the same moves she applies to teens in the book you'll read next. Hearing her say them out loud, in her own warm and slightly stubborn cadence, is the fastest way to load the technique into your head before the page-by-page work.

Chunk

Listen to The Psych Files Episode 135 in full (about thirty minutes). If you'd rather read, the show notes on the episode page summarize the main beats.

Chew

Listen for Faber's four moves for acknowledging a child's feelings: (1) listening with full attention, (2) acknowledging with a single word like "oh" or "mmm," (3) naming the feeling out loud, and (4) giving the kid in fantasy what you can't give them in reality. Notice how each move buys time and lowers the temperature without conceding anything.

Check

Write the four moves on a sticky note in your own words. Put the note somewhere you'll see it before the next hard conversation. The fridge, the inside of your planner, the wall by your classroom door. Somewhere you'll glance at it without meaning to.

2
Step Two · Read · the core text

The cartoon-paneled manual ~40 min

Adele Faber & Elaine Mazlish · How to Talk So Teens Will Listen & Listen So Teens Will Talk · the chapter on dealing with feelings and the chapter on engaging cooperation. Optional companion listen: The Psych Files Episode 136 (Part 2 of the Faber interview).

Faber and Mazlish do an unusual thing in this book. They draw the conversations as cartoon panels, side by side, wrong way and right way. It looks gimmicky and it is not. The panels force you to slow down enough to see how a single rephrasing changes what the kid hears, and how the wrong rephrasing is almost always more natural to reach for.

A script isn't a cage. It's a place to start when the part of you that knows what to do has gone quiet.
Chunk

Read the chapter on dealing with feelings (the foundation for everything else in the book) and the chapter on engaging cooperation. Read the cartoon panels slowly. They are doing more work than they look like they are.

Chew

Find the panel closest to a moment you actually had this past week. Maybe it's the parent who says "don't be ridiculous" when their kid is upset about a friend. Maybe it's the teacher who jumps to advice when a student says they're overwhelmed. Notice exactly what the wrong-way adult does (advice, denial, lecture, comparison, dismissal) and what the right-way adult does (acknowledges the feeling first, then waits).

Check

Pick one recent moment from your own week, classroom or kitchen. Write it out in two columns. Left column: what you actually said. Right column: what you could have said using Faber's acknowledgment moves. Don't make the right column more impressive than the left. Just make it use the moves. The point isn't to feel bad about the left column; it's to give your future self a worked example to copy from.

3
Step Three · Read · the complement

Why intensity is information ~30 min

Lisa Damour · The Emotional Lives of Teenagers · early chapters on the function of emotion, plus the "what to say" sidebars at chapter ends. Optional companion listen: The Social Work Podcast Episode 134 and Episode 135 (a two-part interview with Damour about this book).

Where Faber tells you what to do, Damour tells you why it works. Her central argument in these chapters is that teenage emotional intensity is information about a working system, not evidence of a broken one. That reframe is what gives the Faber moves their backbone. If you don't trust that the feeling is doing something useful, you'll keep trying to talk the kid out of it.

Chunk

Read the early chapters on the function of emotion. Pay particular attention to the "what to say" sidebars and gray boxes Damour places throughout. Those are the lines you can lift directly.

Chew

Underline (or transcribe into a doc) every place where Damour gives you an actual sentence to say. Notice how often those sentences start with an acknowledgment rather than a question, and how often they leave space rather than filling it. Then notice how Damour grounds those sentences in the developmental case: intensity is a sign the emotional system is functioning, not malfunctioning, and the adult job is to be steady scaffolding rather than to dial the experience down.

Check

Draft three short sentences you could actually say in the first thirty seconds of these three situations: (a) a student in tears at your door before first period, (b) a student stonewalling at their desk after a quiz, (c) a student blowing up at a peer in the hallway. Each sentence should be one line. None of them should solve the problem. All of them should pass the test of "would I believe this if it were said to me?"

Draft three short sentences you could actually say in the first thirty seconds of these three situations: (a) they walk in the door already in tears, (b) they stonewall at the dinner table, (c) they blow up at a sibling in the hallway. Each sentence should be one line. None of them should solve the problem. All of them should pass the test of "would I believe this if it were said to me?"

4
Step Four · Optional · the deeper cut

Damour on talking to teens, in her own voice ~45 min

Lisa Damour · "How to Talk to Teenagers" · Everything Happens with Kate Bowler · May 6, 2025 · listen at katebowler.com

If Damour's book felt clinical, this is her in conversation with one of the warmer interviewers working. Bowler is herself a writer about hard things, and the questions she asks pull Damour into the practical end of her own framework. About forty-five minutes. Good for a commute or a long walk.

Chunk

Listen to the full episode, or pick a fifteen-minute segment that maps onto a specific kid you're thinking about this week.

Chew

Listen for the moment Damour separates "emotional" from "fragile." That distinction changes the shape of any adult response. Then listen for the moments where she models the exact sentence you'd say to a teen. Those are the lines worth writing down.

Check

Write one sentence for a substitute folder titled: "If a student gets upset during this period, the first thing to say is…" Don't write the whole crisis plan. Just the first sentence. The one that signals to the kid that the sub is not going to make things worse.

Write one sentence for a spouse, grandparent, or babysitter titled: "If they get upset while I'm out, the first thing to say is…" Don't write the whole crisis plan. Just the first sentence. The one that signals to the kid that this adult is not going to make things worse.

Module Check · The Big Question

Try one of the moves this week

Pick one student or one of your own kids. Try Faber's first move (acknowledging with a word like "oh" or "mmm" before saying anything else) the next time the conversation gets warm. Write down what you said, what they said back, and what changed. Resist the urge to evaluate it as success or failure. The data is just what happened, and the practice is the practice.

The hardest part of this module isn't learning the moves. It's letting yourself trust them when the situation feels urgent and your instincts are screaming to fix the problem. Acknowledging the feeling first feels passive in the moment. It is not passive. It is the only move that opens the door at all, and you can't pry open a door that's been locked by an earlier wrong move.

Module III takes this further, into the situations where the door is already closed. When a student goes silent, when a son shuts down, when nothing you try seems to land. Faber's moves still apply there, but they need an extra layer of patience, and a different theory of what silence is actually saying.
